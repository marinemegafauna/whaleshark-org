Why it matters
Whale sharks are endangered. Photos are how we count them.
You can't tag every shark in the ocean. But a photo of the spots behind the left gills identifies an animal for the rest of its life, without touching it. Enough photos, over enough years, and we can see what a population is doing.

How many are there

Re-sightings of known sharks against new ones tell us population size and whether it is rising or falling at each site.

Where they go

The same shark photographed in Mozambique and Tanzania is a movement record no satellite tag was needed for.

What hurts them

Scars from propellers and nets, recorded to one standard at every site, show whether protection is working.

How it works
Three steps, and you get the shark's story back.
PHOTO · diver photographing left flank, spots visible
01

Photograph the left side

Behind the gills, above the pectoral fin, as square-on as you can. Phone, GoPro or camera all work. Right side is a bonus.

ILLUSTRATION · spot pattern to fingerprint
02

We match the spots

Sharkbook's algorithm compares your shark's pattern with every known whale shark and ranks the closest matches with a confidence score.

SCREENSHOT · shark history page
03

Researchers confirm it

The team at that site checks the match. You get the shark's history, and an email every time it's seen again.

A community resource
Built on Sharkbook. Stewarded by the Marine Megafauna Foundation. Open to everyone.
Sharkbook.ai is Wild Me's open-source Wildbook platform for sharks — the world's largest shark photo-ID database, built over two decades by thousands of contributors. whaleshark.org is a front door to it for whale sharks, and the code is open source so other species can have one too.
